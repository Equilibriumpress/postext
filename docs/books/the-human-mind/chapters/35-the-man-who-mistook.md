# A person is more than a clinical symptom
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat · Oliver Sacks
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Recognition depends on brain processes**

Recognizing a face or object involves more than receiving a visual signal. A person may see features without knowing the whole.

**02 · Personal identity persists amid change**

A difficulty recognizing objects does not erase relationships, preferences or retained skills. Describe the person as more than a clinical deficit.

**03 · Clinical stories need dignity**

Clinical storytelling carries ethical responsibilities. The subject is a person whose voice and dignity should not disappear into a case.

**04 · Perception can separate from sight**

Perception is composed of distinguishable processes. Losing one operation need not eliminate every related capacity.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Memory loss changes everyday life**

Memory changes can reshape routines and relationships. The practical effects depend on support, habits and environment.

**06 · Individual cases resist generalization**

A vivid case shows what is possible, not how frequent a condition is. General conclusions require evidence across many people.

**07 · Adaptations support participation**

Useful adjustments start with what someone still does well. Ask the person and their supporters which changes enable participation.

**08 · Narrative enriches clinical description**

Attention to a life story can add meaning to clinical measurements. Narrative should complement, not replace, careful medical evidence.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Ask about retained strengths**

Before adapting a task, ask what the person does well and which change would help.

**10 · Keep a case separate from averages**

Read a clinical case and write what it shows about that person, not a whole group.
