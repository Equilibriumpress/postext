# Stress, meaning and response
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
The Upside of Stress · Kelly McGonigal
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Stress is not a single experience**

Stress describes several biological and psychological responses, not one fixed state. Duration, control and recovery shape their effects.

**02 · Meaning changes stress appraisal**

Feeling activated before a task does not tell you whether the demand is safe. Separate the body's response from the conditions causing it.

**03 · Social support affects coping**

Social connection can alter how a person copes with a demand. Asking for help is a response to stress, not evidence of weakness.

**04 · Arousal can prepare action**

Elevated arousal sometimes supports quick performance. Chronic overload and unsafe environments require changes beyond a different mindset.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Avoidance can magnify fear**

Avoidance may offer temporary relief while leaving an important challenge unresolved. Approaching a manageable part can create new information.

**06 · Recovery still matters**

Recovery remains necessary even when a task has meaning. Sleep, safety and practical support should never be replaced by positive slogans.

**07 · Evidence differs across settings**

Support from colleagues or a feasible plan can matter more than a slogan about resilience. Name the help available before adding demands.

**08 · Careful reframing avoids denial**

A meaningful cause can sustain effort, yet people differ in the demands they can carry. Values and limits must be considered together.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Separate meaning from risk**

Before a demanding task, write why it matters and which part of the demand is unsafe.

**10 · Plan recovery or a safer adjustment**

Schedule a recovery period after the task. Identify one demand that needs practical change.
