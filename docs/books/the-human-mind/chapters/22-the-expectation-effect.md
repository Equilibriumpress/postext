# Expectations shape perception
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
The Expectation Effect · David Robson
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Expectations change attention**

An expectation draws attention toward certain sensations and outcomes. What people notice can therefore reinforce what they already predict.

**02 · Placebo responses have boundaries**

A placebo response is not evidence that every symptom is imaginary. Effects differ across outcomes and must be studied with suitable controls.

**03 · Mindsets influence perceived effort**

Perceived effort can depend partly on beliefs about an activity. A change in expectation does not eliminate physical limits.

**04 · Beliefs affect symptom interpretation**

An explanation for a bodily sensation can alter its meaning. Persistent symptoms still deserve appropriate professional assessment.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Context shapes expectation**

Social messages and setting can make an expectation more convincing. Context helps explain why the same suggestion has different effects.

**06 · Evidence quality varies by claim**

Results differ across interventions and populations. The book invites consideration of research, not confidence in every claimed effect.

**07 · Reframing need not deny limits**

Replace a catastrophic prediction with a testable one. Keep practical limits, evidence and needed care in view while changing the wording.

**08 · Feedback reshapes future beliefs**

Feedback from an experience updates the next prediction. Track both what was expected and what happened rather than recalling one selectively.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Write a neutral prediction**

Write one prediction about a hard task without assuming failure. Keep its practical limits visible.

**10 · Check prediction against experience**

After the task, compare what happened with the original prediction. Include what stayed difficult.
