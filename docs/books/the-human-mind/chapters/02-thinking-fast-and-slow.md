:::frame{slot="title"}
# Why an easy answer feels right
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
Thinking, Fast and Slow · Daniel Kahneman
:::
:::frame{slot="idea1"}
**01 · Intuition arrives first**

Many impressions form with little conscious effort. These shortcuts help with familiar tasks, yet the feeling of fluency does not prove an answer is accurate.
:::
:::frame{slot="idea2"}
**02 · Attention has a price**

A second judgment takes attention, time and a reason to question the first. More effort does not guarantee accuracy when the same weak evidence is reused.
:::
:::frame{slot="idea3"}
**03 · The first number becomes an anchor**

An opening price or estimate influences later guesses. An arbitrary starting point still has an effect unless another reference is deliberately considered.
:::
:::frame{slot="idea4"}
**04 · Vivid is not common**

A memorable example comes to mind quickly. Availability makes an event seem frequent even when representative statistics tell a different story.
:::
:::frame{slot="idea5"}
**05 · Losses change preferences**

The perceived pain of losing often exceeds the appeal of an equal gain. The size of the effect depends on the decision and its context.
:::
:::frame{slot="idea6"}
**06 · Descriptions alter choices**

Identical outcomes described as survival or mortality may prompt different responses. Reframing the same facts exposes the influence of presentation.
:::
:::frame{slot="idea7"}
**07 · Begin with the base rate**

Before interpreting one person's case, ask how common the outcome is in the relevant group. A base rate becomes useful when the groups are comparable.
:::
:::frame{slot="idea8"}
**08 · Confidence needs calibration**

Detailed explanations are persuasive, including to the person who produces them. Check predictions against results to understand the limits of certainty.
:::
:::frame{slot="practicehead"}
## Two things to try
:::
:::frame{slot="practice1"}
**09 · Estimate value before seeing a price**

Before seeing an asking price, write two estimates using separate comparable bicycles.
:::
:::frame{slot="practice2"}
**10 · Reverse the frame**

Write the same choice once as a possible gain and once as a possible loss. Compare your preference.
