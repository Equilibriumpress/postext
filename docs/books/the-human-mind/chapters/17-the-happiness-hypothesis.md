# Ancient questions, modern evidence
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
The Happiness Hypothesis · Jonathan Haidt
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Reason and intuition interact**

Haidt's rider-and-elephant image presents reasoning and intuition as interacting forces. The image is illustrative, not an anatomical division.

**02 · Relationships matter to wellbeing**

An immediate reward may fade while an ongoing relationship supplies a different kind of value. Both experiences depend on a person's circumstances.

**03 · Adversity has varied consequences**

Hardship does not automatically produce growth. Circumstances and support influence what people take from a difficult experience.

**04 · Virtues shape daily practice**

Repeated actions shape character over time. Virtue traditions propose practices that can be evaluated in the present rather than accepted whole.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Purpose connects personal actions**

Meaning connects personal aims to something larger than immediate reward. People construct that connection through different commitments.

**06 · Happiness includes social context**

Wellbeing arises within communities and institutions. Material security and interpersonal safety remain relevant alongside personal attitudes.

**07 · Moral thought has emotional foundations**

Some people find meaning after adversity, but suffering is not a required path to growth. Avoid treating hardship as a benefit owed to its survivor.

**08 · Wisdom needs multiple perspectives**

Historical traditions disagree about flourishing. Comparing them offers questions and possibilities rather than a single universal recipe.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Identify one sustaining relationship**

Describe one recent conversation that offered trust or help. Name what made it meaningful.

**10 · Thank someone for one specific act**

For three evenings, note one kind act you received. Thank the person when appropriate.
