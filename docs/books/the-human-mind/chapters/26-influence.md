# Seven cues behind a yes
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
Influence · Robert B. Cialdini
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Reciprocity creates obligation**

A gift or favor may encourage a return gesture. The original kindness does not obligate a person to accept an unrelated request.

**02 · Commitment encourages consistency**

A past public commitment may make a later yes feel consistent. Before agreeing again, ask whether the new request still serves the original purpose.

**03 · Social proof offers a shortcut**

Other people's actions help under uncertainty. A crowd can also repeat the same mistake or respond to fabricated popularity.

**04 · Authority influences decisions**

Credentials and roles can signal expertise. Check whether an authority is relevant to the particular claim being made.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Liking softens resistance**

Similarity, familiarity and personal warmth affect receptiveness. Warmth and evidence should still be evaluated separately.

**06 · Scarcity increases urgency**

Limited availability makes an option feel valuable. Pressure to decide quickly should trigger a check of the underlying value.

**07 · Unity signals shared identity**

A shared identity can make an invitation persuasive, especially in the expanded edition. Belonging offers no independent proof of the claim.

**08 · Persuasion has ethical boundaries**

Social influence occurs in ordinary life. Fair use involves clear information, voluntary choice and respect for refusal.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Assess value without urgency**

Write down the value of a limited-time offer without mentioning its deadline or popularity.

**10 · Check one authority claim**

Select one claim from a trusted speaker. Verify its underlying evidence with another source.
