# No single typical brain
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
The Neuroscience of You · Chantel Prat
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Neural variation is expected**

Variation is normal across brains and cognition. An average summarizes a group but says little about a particular person's abilities.

**02 · Attention differs across people**

Attention varies by task, history and circumstance as well as between people. A preferred activity does not identify an immutable learning type.

**03 · Perception depends on prior learning**

Prior knowledge and sensory experience alter perception. Two people can reasonably report different impressions of one scene.

**04 · Language networks vary**

Language uses interacting networks whose patterns vary between people. Simple left-brain versus right-brain stereotypes do not help.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Experience shapes connectivity**

Learning and experience shape functional organization. This does not imply that every capability can be changed without constraint.

**06 · Group averages hide individuals**

Group-level findings can hide considerable overlap between individuals. A small statistical average should not define a personal trait.

**07 · No brain type defines worth**

No single score or brain image determines a person's value or abilities. Individual predictions need relevant evidence and meaningful uncertainty.

**08 · Claims need careful interpretation**

A brain measurement requires an interpretation and a question. Avoid turning a vivid scan image into a personal diagnosis.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Compare methods, not brain types**

Try two learning methods on similar tasks and compare errors or recall, not identity labels.

**10 · Check overlap behind averages**

Find a study comparing groups and check whether their individual results overlap.
