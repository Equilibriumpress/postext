:::frame{slot="title"}
# When attention excludes the unexpected
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
The Invisible Gorilla · Christopher Chabris and Daniel Simons
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Focused attention excludes other events**

Looking closely at one task can make an unexpected event invisible. This is selective attention, not simply a failure to look carefully.

**02 · Confidence in awareness can mislead**

After watching a scene, confidence in noticing everything is not an independent test. A second observer looking for another feature may find what you missed.

**03 · Change blindness hides edits**

A change across two views can escape detection when attention follows another element. The missing difference is not proof of carelessness.

**04 · Memory rewrites observed details**

Recollection is reconstructed rather than replayed. An eyewitness may remember a coherent story that omits or alters a detail.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Multitasking weakens noticing**

Switching between tasks interrupts the sustained focus that difficult observation needs. The precise cost depends on the tasks involved.

**06 · Expertise does not prevent omissions**

Training helps people know what to look for, yet expertise also creates expectations. Unexpected evidence can still be overlooked.

**07 · Looking directly is not enough**

Eye direction does not prove conscious awareness. Looking at an area and later reporting what happened there are separate observations.

**08 · Expectation directs perception**

Expectation directs limited attention towards predicted events. Deliberately seeking anomalies is useful when the stakes are high.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Ask what the observation omitted**

After reviewing a scene, name one detail you focused on and one category you did not check.

**10 · Check without switching tasks**

For one important check, mute messages and finish the observation before switching tasks.
