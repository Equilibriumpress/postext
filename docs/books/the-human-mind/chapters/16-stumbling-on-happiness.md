:::frame{slot="title"}
# Why tomorrow's happiness is hard to forecast
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
Stumbling on Happiness · Daniel Gilbert
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Imagination leaves out details**

When imagining a future event, people leave out ordinary details. That sketch can make an anticipated joy or disappointment seem all-consuming.

**02 · Present emotion colors forecasts**

A forecast highlights what is easy to picture. Ask which ordinary routines will continue after the big change and what those routines cost.

**03 · Adaptation changes later experience**

Adaptation changes the intensity of an experience over time. People underestimate how routines and attention will move on.

**04 · Memory edits past satisfaction**

Memory selects peaks and endings rather than replaying every moment. Its version of a happy day may differ from the day itself.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Similar experiences offer clues**

Someone who has already lived through a comparable choice can offer useful information. Their experience is still one sample, not a guarantee.

**06 · Certainty is often misplaced**

Forecasters often express more certainty than their information warrants. Treat predictions of emotional intensity as rough estimates.

**07 · Comparisons shift preferences**

Talk to people living with a similar choice and ask about a normal weekday, not only their best moment. Their answers are information, not predictions.

**08 · Happiness is context sensitive**

Wellbeing depends on context, social ties and changing expectations. A single possession or outcome cannot settle the question of happiness.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Ask about an ordinary weekday**

Ask someone who made a similar choice what an ordinary weekday now involves.

**10 · Revisit a forecast after a month**

Write one prediction now. Revisit it after a month and note which routine details you overlooked.
