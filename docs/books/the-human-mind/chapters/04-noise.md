# Why equally qualified judges disagree
:::
:::frame{slot="metadata"}
Noise · Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass R. Sunstein
:::
:::frame{slot="left"}
**01 · Bias and noise are different errors**

Bias is a repeated tilt in one direction. Noise is the spread of judgments that should agree, and the two can occur together.

**02 · Judges disagree on similar cases**

Compare judgments only when reviewers face genuinely comparable cases and criteria. Disagreement on different questions is not automatically unwanted noise.

**03 · Mood can alter a judgment**

A judgment made after a difficult day may differ from one made under calm conditions. Institutions rarely measure these shifts.

**04 · Sequence affects assessments**

When cases arrive in a particular order, preceding cases can influence the next rating. Shuffle or isolate inputs where possible.
:::
:::frame{slot="right"}
**05 · Structured criteria reduce variation**

Clear decision criteria keep irrelevant details from driving evaluations. A checklist is not enough unless users apply it consistently.

**06 · Independent judgments avoid group anchoring**

Independent estimates preserve useful disagreement before a group conversation creates pressure to align with the first speaker.

**07 · Decision hygiene improves consistency**

Decision hygiene preserves independent ratings and checks shared criteria before discussion. Its value depends on a domain where consistency matters.

**08 · Measure disagreement before fixing it**

Record the spread of estimates before designing a remedy. Average agreement alone can hide systematic differences between evaluators.
:::
:::frame{slot="applications"}
## Two things to try

**09 · Compare two independent ratings**

Give two reviewers the same anonymous case and criteria. Record both scores before discussion.

**10 · Set the criteria before reading**

Write three scoring criteria before opening a case. Define what high and low scores mean.
