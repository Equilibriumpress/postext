# 25. Social {#rl-social}

*Matthew D. Lieberman · 2013 · The Social Mind*

**Social interaction is an important dimension of human cognition.**

::resource{id="thm-social"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

On a volunteer's first day, a colleague introduces her to the team and demonstrates how visitors are welcomed. Later, the volunteer asks why a visitor seemed upset. A familiar routine, a sense of belonging and an attempt to understand another perspective all play different roles in the same shift.

## What distinguishes this book

Matthew Lieberman connects social relationships, belonging and aspects of social cognition to neuroscientific research. His contribution is to treat social experience as central to everyday thought, rather than an optional addition to individual intelligence.

## What the claim does not establish

Shared activity in brain regions does not mean that rejection and physical pain are identical. Individuals differ in the relationships they need and the meaning of social encounters.

> **Read alongside The Extended Mind.** Annie Murphy Paul shows how another person's knowledge can support problem solving. Lieberman looks more broadly at social connection and social cognition. Being supported by people is not the same as having every social need met.

## Eight ideas

1. **Belonging affects wellbeing** People monitor social belonging because relationships affect everyday safety and support. The needs of individuals are not identical.

2. **Rejection can feel threatening** Rejection can produce a strong social response. Comparing it with physical pain requires care: shared measures do not mean identical experiences.

3. **Perspective taking requires work** Perspective taking uses inferences about another person's thoughts. Accuracy improves when those inferences are checked in conversation.

4. **Social learning changes behavior** Learning from others reduces the need to test every action alone. Social learning also spreads misinformation.

5. **Status shapes interactions** Status and recognition influence interactions. They are part of the context, not complete explanations of behavior.

6. **Mentalizing helps cooperation** Mentalizing supports cooperation by making goals and intentions thinkable. It does not guarantee we interpret another person correctly.

7. **Identity emerges through relationships** Relationships contribute to how people describe themselves. No one encounter or group membership defines the entirety of a person.

8. **Brain findings require measured claims** Neuroscience helps investigate social processes but cannot resolve every social question by a brain image. Behavior and context still matter.

## Two things to try

1. **Check a guess about another person** Recall a recent choice by someone else. Write what you assume motivated it, then ask the person about their goal and compare the answer with your initial account.

2. **Plan regular meaningful contact** Choose a person with whom you would like more contact. Suggest a realistic recurring call, walk or shared activity and ask whether the timing suits them.
