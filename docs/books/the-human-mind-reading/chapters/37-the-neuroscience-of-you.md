# 30. The Neuroscience of You {#rl-the-neuroscience-of-you}

*Chantel Prat · 2022 · The Brain & The Self*

**Individual differences matter more than flattering brain labels.**

::resource{id="thm-the-neuroscience-of-you"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

A report compares two groups described as analytical and intuitive. Their average scores differ, but the distributions overlap and several members of each group resemble members of the other. The pattern invites a research question, not a label to pin on any individual.

## What distinguishes this book

Chantel Prat explores differences between brains and questions the appeal of simple cognitive types. Her distinctive contribution is to bring individual variation into view when a headline or brain image tempts a reader to assume one kind of mind fits everyone.

## What the claim does not establish

A group average, test score or scan requires context, uncertainty and suitable validation. It cannot establish a fixed 'brain type' or determine a person's ability, worth or future.

> **Read alongside The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat.** Sacks's portrait warns against reducing someone to a clinical feature. Prat warns against reducing someone to a group average. Both books leave room for individual histories that a single measure cannot capture.

## Eight ideas

1. **Neural variation is expected** Variation is normal across brains and cognition. An average summarizes a group but says little about a particular person's abilities.

2. **Attention differs across people** Attention varies by task, history and circumstance as well as between people. A preferred activity does not identify an immutable learning type.

3. **Perception depends on prior learning** Prior knowledge and sensory experience alter perception. Two people can reasonably report different impressions of one scene.

4. **Language networks vary** Language uses interacting networks whose patterns vary between people. Simple left-brain versus right-brain stereotypes do not help.

5. **Experience shapes connectivity** Learning and experience shape functional organization. This does not imply that every capability can be changed without constraint.

6. **Group averages hide individuals** Group-level findings can hide considerable overlap between individuals. A small statistical average should not define a personal trait.

7. **No brain type defines worth** No single score or brain image determines a person's value or abilities. Individual predictions need relevant evidence and meaningful uncertainty.

8. **Claims need careful interpretation** A brain measurement requires an interpretation and a question. Avoid turning a vivid scan image into a personal diagnosis.

## Two things to try

1. **Compare methods, not brain types** Use two comparable short tasks and alternate between two study approaches. Compare the results you actually observe without calling yourself a fixed visual, analytical or other brain type.

2. **Check overlap behind averages** When reading a report of average group differences, look for the spread and overlap of individual results. Write what the average alone cannot predict about one person.
