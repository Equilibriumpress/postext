# 10. Why Don't Students Like School? {#rl-why-dont-students-like-school}

*Daniel T. Willingham · 2009 · Memory & Learning*

**Working memory is small, but knowledge changes what fits inside it.**

::resource{id="thm-why-dont-students-like-school"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

A teacher writes three quarters on the board. Several pupils know the notation but do not know what fraction of an object it describes. Paper strips, contrasting examples and a short explanation give them a starting point for the more abstract exercise that follows.

## What distinguishes this book

Daniel Willingham places background knowledge and working-memory demands at the center of effective instruction. A learner cannot reason freely about a topic without enough relevant knowledge to hold its pieces together.

## What the claim does not establish

Examples and practice need adaptation to prior knowledge. The argument does not establish a single teaching method for every child and offers no support for assigning people fixed learning styles.

> **Read alongside Make It Stick.** Make It Stick explains why retrieving and spacing known material can strengthen learning. Willingham asks what must already be understood before retrieval practice becomes useful. For a novice, providing an example may come before closing the book.

## Eight ideas

1. **Background knowledge aids comprehension** Relevant background knowledge lets a reader infer relationships that remain obscure to a beginner. Comprehension is more than decoding words.

2. **Memory supports complex reasoning** New information competes for limited working memory. Reduce simultaneous demands without removing the underlying intellectual challenge.

3. **Attention favors meaningful problems** A carefully framed problem attracts attention when the learner sees a path to an answer. Confusion without a foothold discourages thought.

4. **Practice makes basic skills automatic** Repeated foundational practice makes some operations faster. This leaves attention for meaning rather than every basic step.

5. **Understanding needs examples** Abstract rules are clearer when grounded in several contrasting examples. A single example is easy to copy without understanding.

6. **Working memory has limits** Instruction should keep the number of simultaneously new demands manageable. Simplifying a task is not the same as reducing its ambition.

7. **Feedback supports expertise** Feedback should identify the step or assumption that failed. More practice without that information may repeat the same mistake.

8. **Mental effort depends on task design** The difficulty of a task depends on what a person already knows. Adapt teaching to the learner's present knowledge, not an assumed style.

## Two things to try

1. **Teach through contrasting examples** Introduce a new idea with two carefully contrasting examples. Ask the learner to explain the difference in their own words before presenting a third example to classify.

2. **Practice a prerequisite first** Find one prerequisite that makes a harder task difficult. Practice that step briefly, check errors immediately and revisit the original task to see whether the next step becomes manageable.
