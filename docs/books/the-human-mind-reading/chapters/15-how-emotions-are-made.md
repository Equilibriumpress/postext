# 11. How Emotions Are Made {#rl-how-emotions-are-made}

*Lisa Feldman Barrett · 2017 · Emotions & Happiness*

**One influential theory treats emotions as situated constructions.**

::resource{id="thm-how-emotions-are-made"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

Two people leave demanding interviews with a racing pulse. One describes anticipation; the other describes fear. Their sensations overlap, while their memories of the interviews and expectations about the outcome differ. This invented contrast illustrates a theory, not an experiment.

[Context reference](https://lisafeldmanbarrett.com/books/how-emotions-are-made/)

## What distinguishes this book

Lisa Feldman Barrett challenges the idea that facial expressions or bodily signals reveal fixed emotion programs. Her theory emphasizes predictions, learned concepts and bodily sensations in the construction of an emotional episode.

## What the claim does not establish

Emotion construction is a research program with competing accounts. Similar bodily sensations do not prove the theory, and a brief invented scene cannot establish how an individual's feeling formed.

> **Read alongside Emotional Agility.** Barrett asks what processes help produce an emotional experience. Susan David asks how to choose a response once a feeling is present. The first is an explanatory theory, the second a practical framework; neither validates the other.

## Eight ideas

1. **Emotions are not simple readouts** Barrett proposes that an emotion is constructed in a particular situation. This contrasts with accounts centered on fixed emotion programs.

2. **Prediction has a role** Bodily sensations are information, but a racing heart has many possible causes. Context helps a person interpret what they notice.

3. **Body signals contribute** Heartbeat, breathing and other bodily changes inform the experience of feeling. A sensation alone does not uniquely identify an emotion.

4. **Context shifts meaning** A racing heart might accompany anticipation, exertion or fear. The surrounding circumstances help a person interpret the sensation.

5. **Concepts shape categories** People learn words and categories for experiences. Those concepts influence how they label, recognize and remember emotional episodes.

6. **Faces need interpretation** A facial expression cannot always be translated into one emotion without context. People and cultures vary in expressive behavior.

7. **Different theories coexist** An emotion label reflects learned concepts and circumstances. Different labels do not by themselves establish different underlying brain mechanisms.

8. **Description expands choices** Separating bodily signals, thoughts and surroundings creates a more precise vocabulary. A description need not dictate the next action.

## Two things to try

1. **Observe before labeling a feeling** After an everyday emotional moment, describe two bodily sensations and three observable features of the situation. Only then name the feeling, noting that the label is an interpretation.

2. **Compare two possible interpretations** Return to the same episode and write two plausible explanations for what you felt. Name evidence for and against each without treating either as a diagnosis.
