# 02. The Invisible Gorilla {#rl-the-invisible-gorilla}

*Christopher Chabris and Daniel Simons · 2010 · How We Think*

**Selective attention creates blind spots without announcing them.**

::resource{id="thm-the-invisible-gorilla"}

## In context

*DOCUMENTED EXAMPLE*

Volunteers watch a film of players passing basketballs and count the passes made by one team. An unexpected figure in a gorilla costume walks across the scene. Some viewers later report no gorilla at all. This is a documented attention task: the unseen event was visible, but not always noticed.

[Research/case source](https://theinvisiblegorilla.com/blog/2010/02/26/behind-the-scenes-of-the-%E2%80%9Cgorilla-experiment%E2%80%9D/)

## What distinguishes this book

Chabris and Simons show a gap between seeing what reaches our eyes and noticing what enters awareness. The demonstration is memorable because observers often expect themselves to catch the unexpected. Their wider argument questions that confidence, rather than claiming human vision is generally defective.

## What the claim does not establish

The experiment concerns particular tasks and participants. It does not establish that every observer misses surprises, nor that every attention lapse can be explained by the same mechanism.

> **Read alongside The Scout Mindset.** A missed visual event and a defended belief are different kinds of blind spot. The Invisible Gorilla asks what attention never registers. The Scout Mindset asks whether a person revises a belief after conflicting information becomes available.

## Eight ideas

1. **Focused attention excludes other events** Looking closely at one task can make an unexpected event invisible. This is selective attention, not simply a failure to look carefully.

2. **Confidence in awareness can mislead** After watching a scene, confidence in noticing everything is not an independent test. A second observer looking for another feature may find what you missed.

3. **Change blindness hides edits** A change across two views can escape detection when attention follows another element. The missing difference is not proof of carelessness.

4. **Memory rewrites observed details** Recollection is reconstructed rather than replayed. An eyewitness may remember a coherent story that omits or alters a detail.

5. **Multitasking weakens noticing** Switching between tasks interrupts the sustained focus that difficult observation needs. The precise cost depends on the tasks involved.

6. **Expertise does not prevent omissions** Training helps people know what to look for, yet expertise also creates expectations. Unexpected evidence can still be overlooked.

7. **Looking directly is not enough** Eye direction does not prove conscious awareness. Looking at an area and later reporting what happened there are separate observations.

8. **Expectation directs perception** Expectation directs limited attention towards predicted events. Deliberately seeking anomalies is useful when the stakes are high.

## Two things to try

1. **Ask what the observation omitted** Review a familiar photo or short scene for a specific target. Write down what you noticed, then ask another observer to look for a different kind of detail and compare your notes.

2. **Check without switching tasks** Choose a task where missing a detail would matter. Set a short uninterrupted observation period, turn off notifications and document what you checked before opening messages again.
