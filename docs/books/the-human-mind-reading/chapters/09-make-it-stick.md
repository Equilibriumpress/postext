# 06. Make It Stick {#rl-make-it-stick}

*Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III and Mark A. McDaniel · 2014 · Memory & Learning*

**Durable learning needs retrieval and spacing.**

::resource{id="thm-make-it-stick"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

A student reads the same history page until its sentences feel effortless. The following morning, she cannot explain the events without looking. On another topic she closes the book, retrieves what she remembers and checks each error. The second approach feels slower but reveals what remains available later.

## What distinguishes this book

Brown, Roediger and McDaniel distinguish familiarity from retrieval. Drawing on research into practice testing, spacing and mixed practice, they show why a task that feels demanding can sometimes support later recall better than a comfortable repetition.

## What the claim does not establish

Not every difficulty is desirable. Retrieval, spacing and interleaving depend on the material, the learner's preparation and access to feedback. Frustration without correction is not a teaching strategy.

> **Read alongside Remember.** Make It Stick asks which study choices improve later retrieval. Lisa Genova's Remember broadens the question to how memories are encoded, recovered and forgotten in everyday life. A study tip cannot explain every lapse of memory.

## Eight ideas

1. **Pull knowledge from memory** Trying to answer without notes strengthens later retrieval. A short quiz used for practice is different from a high-pressure assessment.

2. **Space the repetitions** Space attempts over time instead of repeating everything at once. Compare recall after a delay, not only how confident you feel at the end of study.

3. **Interleave related skills** Switching between problem types makes practice feel harder. The learner must identify the appropriate method instead of repeating one routine.

4. **Fluency can mislead** Freshly reread text feels familiar, yet that feeling does not demonstrate future recall. Test comprehension away from the page.

5. **Desirable difficulty has limits** Effort sometimes improves retention when a learner has adequate foundations and feedback. Overwhelming confusion is not a learning strategy.

6. **Feedback repairs mistakes** Checking an attempted answer identifies misconceptions. Feedback works best when it tells the learner how to improve the next attempt.

7. **Explain links in your own words** Explain a new idea without copying a source sentence. Then check whether your explanation connects the right causes, steps and examples.

8. **Reflect after practice** Describe the strategy used, what failed and what to try next. This makes a lesson more useful across unfamiliar tasks.

## Two things to try

1. **Close the book, then retrieve** Read a short passage, close the source and answer three questions without notes. Mark errors with a different pen after checking the page, then attempt the same questions the next day.

2. **Space a second and third attempt** Choose the same three questions from your first attempt. Test yourself tomorrow and again several days later without looking first. Compare errors rather than judging how familiar the page feels.
