# 16. Atomic Habits {#rl-atomic-habits}

*James Clear · 2018 · Habits & Motivation*

**Behavior repeats through cues, responses and rewards.**

::resource{id="thm-atomic-habits"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

A reader plans to open a book each evening but keeps the book inside a cupboard while a phone charges beside the bed. Moving the book to the pillow and the phone across the room changes what is easiest to reach. The change does not create extra time, but it changes the start of the routine.

## What distinguishes this book

James Clear presents habits as repeated behaviors shaped by cues, rewards and their surrounding environment. His distinctive contribution is making a desired action easier to begin and an unwanted one less convenient, without assuming motivation will stay constant.

## What the claim does not establish

The habit loop is a useful design account, not a universal explanation of behavior. Work, illness, resources and social demands affect which routines are realistic.

> **Read alongside Tiny Habits.** Clear emphasizes environmental friction and an accessible start. Fogg specifies a tiny behavior tied to an existing prompt and often includes celebration. Both adjust action design, but their frameworks should not be presented as interchangeable experiments.

## Eight ideas

1. **Identity gives a direction** A goal defines an outcome. A repeated action also provides evidence for the kind of person someone wants to become.

2. **Make the cue obvious** Put a reliable cue where the desired action happens. A visible book invites reading, but the cue cannot guarantee time or attention.

3. **Connect it to a routine** An existing daily event supplies a dependable starting point. An after-this-then-that plan is often more precise than an intention.

4. **Lower the first-step cost** Preparing tools and making a two-minute version reduces startup friction. Ease does not replace the need for later practice.

5. **Make the action attractive** Pair a useful task with something enjoyable where appropriate. Repetition grows easier when the experience is tolerable.

6. **Use an immediate signal** A simple completion mark makes progress visible. The reward should reinforce the action rather than overwhelm the original purpose.

7. **Shape the surroundings** Change one feature of the surroundings before demanding more willpower. Check whether the change reduces effort in your actual routine.

8. **Restart without drama** A missed day is information about the routine. Adapt the plan and return to the smallest version rather than waiting for a perfect restart.

## Two things to try

1. **Design a two-minute starting action** Pick one desired behavior and reduce the first step to two minutes, such as opening a book to one page. Try the starter today and record whether the opening step was realistically easy.

2. **Change one environmental cue** Change the setting for that routine: make the desired tool visible and place one competing distraction out of reach. Observe for a week whether starting becomes easier.
