# 01. Thinking, Fast and Slow {#rl-thinking-fast-and-slow}

*Daniel Kahneman · 2011 · How We Think*

**Fast and effortful thinking shape judgments.**

::resource{id="thm-thinking-fast-and-slow"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

The seller begins with a high price for a second-hand bicycle. A buyer spots worn brakes but continues to bargain around the seller's figure. Only after checking other bicycles does the buyer notice how little independent evidence supported that first number. The scene illustrates anchoring, not its frequency or size.

## What distinguishes this book

Kahneman joins several ways judgment can mislead us: anchoring, framing, neglect of background frequencies and misplaced confidence. His fast-and-slow vocabulary helps describe when thought feels effortless and when scrutiny requires attention. It does not turn every snap judgment into an error.

## What the claim does not establish

Two systems are explanatory labels, not separate anatomical parts of the brain. Particular findings have different levels of replication and depend on how each experiment is designed.

> **Read alongside Noise.** A person's estimate can be anchored while several experts still disagree for unrelated reasons. Thinking, Fast and Slow focuses on recurring judgment patterns. Noise measures unwanted variation across decisions. One problem cannot be diagnosed from evidence of the other.

## Eight ideas

1. **Intuition arrives first** Many impressions form with little conscious effort. These shortcuts help with familiar tasks, yet the feeling of fluency does not prove an answer is accurate.

2. **Attention has a price** A second judgment takes attention, time and a reason to question the first. More effort does not guarantee accuracy when the same weak evidence is reused.

3. **The first number becomes an anchor** An opening price or estimate influences later guesses. An arbitrary starting point still has an effect unless another reference is deliberately considered.

4. **Vivid is not common** A memorable example comes to mind quickly. Availability makes an event seem frequent even when representative statistics tell a different story.

5. **Losses change preferences** The perceived pain of losing often exceeds the appeal of an equal gain. The size of the effect depends on the decision and its context.

6. **Descriptions alter choices** Identical outcomes described as survival or mortality may prompt different responses. Reframing the same facts exposes the influence of presentation.

7. **Begin with the base rate** Before interpreting one person's case, ask how common the outcome is in the relevant group. A base rate becomes useful when the groups are comparable.

8. **Confidence needs calibration** Detailed explanations are persuasive, including to the person who produces them. Check predictions against results to understand the limits of certainty.

## Two things to try

1. **Estimate value before seeing a price** Find two comparable bicycle listings without looking at the seller's asking price. Estimate a value from each, record both numbers, then reveal the asking price and note any change to your valuation.

2. **Reverse the frame** Describe one decision using what you would gain, then describe the identical outcome using what you might lose. Keep the numbers unchanged and note whether your preference shifts.
