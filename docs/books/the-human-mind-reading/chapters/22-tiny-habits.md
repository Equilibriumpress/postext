# 17. Tiny Habits {#rl-tiny-habits}

*BJ Fogg · 2019 · Habits & Motivation*

**For BJ Fogg, a behavior meets ability, motivation and a prompt.**

::resource{id="thm-tiny-habits"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

Every morning, a person puts water in a kettle and then waters one plant. The task takes seconds and follows a predictable event. On a rushed morning it still fits. The person notices the small completion rather than waiting for a dramatic result. This illustrates Fogg's practice, not a measured habit outcome.

[Context reference](https://tinyhabitsacademy.com/fogg-behavior-model/)

## What distinguishes this book

BJ Fogg describes behavior as the meeting of motivation, ability and a prompt. His practical method starts with an extremely manageable action attached to a reliable daily event, then experiments with the design if repetition fails.

## What the claim does not establish

Celebrating small success belongs to Fogg's method, but does not prove automatic habit formation or lasting benefit for everyone. The right action depends on context and constraints.

> **Read alongside Atomic Habits.** Both books reduce the difficulty of starting. Fogg puts particular weight on a prompt and an intentionally tiny behavior; Clear gives more attention to the environment around the desired routine. Neither is a substitute for addressing an impossible workload.

## Eight ideas

1. **Ability interacts with motivation** The Fogg Behavior Model brings motivation, ability and a prompt into one moment. If an action never happens, examine all three.

2. **Prompt design matters** A prompt must appear when the action is feasible. Put the new step after an existing event rather than depending on a vague intention.

3. **Tiny actions reduce resistance** An action as small as opening a notebook removes much of the resistance to starting. The small step is a beginning, not a full outcome.

4. **Celebration marks success** A positive feeling at the end of a completed behavior can support repetition. The celebration should feel sincere rather than forced.

5. **Anchors supply predictable timing** Anchoring a new behavior to an event already in the day provides a cue. A vague plan lacks that reliable place.

6. **Growth starts with repetition** Repeated success can make a routine more available. Growth depends on whether the behavior remains useful in the person's context.

7. **Behavior design needs experiments** If the routine fails, change its timing, size or prompt and observe the result. A failed design is information, not a measure of character.

8. **Adjust the behavior to real life** An effective routine fits real constraints. A behavior that works during a quiet week may need resizing during a demanding month.

## Two things to try

1. **Choose a ten-second action** Select an action so small you can do it on a difficult day, such as opening a notebook or placing a cup beside the sink. Perform only that step and note whether its size is feasible.

2. **Anchor an action to a reliable event** Choose a consistent daily event, such as switching on a kettle. Say, 'After I do this, I will water one plant,' then perform the action and notice its completion. Adjust the anchor if it proves unreliable.
