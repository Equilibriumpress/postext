# 05. How Minds Change {#rl-how-minds-change}

*David McRaney · 2022 · How We Think*

**Curiosity and connection precede persuasion in many conversations.**

::resource{id="thm-how-minds-change"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

At a family meal, two relatives argue about phones in classrooms. One repeats statistics; the other explains a past incident that made the issue feel urgent. The conversation shifts when they ask what each person hopes to protect, even though neither changes their conclusion that evening.

## What distinguishes this book

David McRaney examines why beliefs resist correction and how curiosity, social identity and conversation shape opportunities for revision. His emphasis is on the conditions under which people examine their reasoning, not a script that reliably persuades everyone.

## What the claim does not establish

Listening does not prove a disputed claim true, nor does it guarantee agreement. Sometimes the other person has sound reasons to retain a position after hearing a different one.

> **Read alongside The Scout Mindset.** McRaney concentrates on the conversation between people. Galef concentrates on the standards by which one person updates confidence. Combining them makes sense only when careful listening is paired with a willingness to evaluate actual evidence.

## Eight ideas

1. **Facts alone seldom shift identity beliefs** Facts can be dismissed when they threaten a group identity. More information is not automatically more influence.

2. **A question invites reflection** Ask a sincere question before supplying a correction. A question helps reveal the premise behind a belief, but the response still needs evidence.

3. **Curiosity is better than accusation** Direct accusations prompt people to defend themselves. An open-ended conversation gives room for uncertainty.

4. **People explain their own reasoning** When people describe their own reasoning, they sometimes spot a contradiction themselves. The insight belongs to the speaker.

5. **Social belonging shapes opinion** Belonging influences which sources seem trustworthy. A factual correction rarely addresses the social cost of switching sides.

6. **Respect reduces defensive reactions** Respectful listening does not require agreement. It lowers the chance that a disagreement becomes a contest over dignity.

7. **Listen for underlying values** A disagreement about facts may express concern about dignity, safety or fairness. Identify the concern before arguing about the interpretation of evidence.

8. **Changes often take multiple encounters** Some changes occur through many experiences rather than one decisive conversation. Do not measure success by immediate conversion.

## Two things to try

1. **Ask how someone formed a view** In a respectful conversation, invite another person to describe the experience or evidence behind one view. Listen to the answer without interruption and repeat what you heard before responding.

2. **Find the question behind a dispute** After listening, propose one neutral question that both participants agree is the real disagreement. Ask each person what evidence or concern would help them answer it.
