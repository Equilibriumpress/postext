# 13. Stumbling on Happiness {#rl-stumbling-on-happiness}

*Daniel Gilbert · 2006 · Emotions & Happiness*

**The mind fills gaps in its pictures of future experience.**

::resource{id="thm-stumbling-on-happiness"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

Before moving to a coastal town, a writer pictures the sea outside her window and predicts lasting delight. Six months later, friendships, costs and a long commute fill most weekdays. The sea still matters, but it is one part of an ordinary life that the original forecast left out.

## What distinguishes this book

Daniel Gilbert studies the mistakes people make while predicting their future experiences. Our forecasts often zoom in on the striking change and leave out the ordinary routines that will surround it.

## What the claim does not establish

Affective forecasting findings vary by task and person. The example does not show that a sea view has no lasting value or that people cannot make sensible long-term plans.

> **Read alongside The Happiness Hypothesis.** Gilbert asks why imagined future happiness diverges from experience. Haidt asks which relationships, practices and conditions support a good life. Misforecasting one choice does not answer the larger question of wellbeing.

## Eight ideas

1. **Imagination leaves out details** When imagining a future event, people leave out ordinary details. That sketch can make an anticipated joy or disappointment seem all-consuming.

2. **Present emotion colors forecasts** A forecast highlights what is easy to picture. Ask which ordinary routines will continue after the big change and what those routines cost.

3. **Adaptation changes later experience** Adaptation changes the intensity of an experience over time. People underestimate how routines and attention will move on.

4. **Memory edits past satisfaction** Memory selects peaks and endings rather than replaying every moment. Its version of a happy day may differ from the day itself.

5. **Similar experiences offer clues** Someone who has already lived through a comparable choice can offer useful information. Their experience is still one sample, not a guarantee.

6. **Certainty is often misplaced** Forecasters often express more certainty than their information warrants. Treat predictions of emotional intensity as rough estimates.

7. **Comparisons shift preferences** Talk to people living with a similar choice and ask about a normal weekday, not only their best moment. Their answers are information, not predictions.

8. **Happiness is context sensitive** Wellbeing depends on context, social ties and changing expectations. A single possession or outcome cannot settle the question of happiness.

## Two things to try

1. **Ask about an ordinary weekday** Before a significant choice, ask two people with comparable experience what an ordinary weekday is like after the change. Record routines, costs and relationships that your forecast omitted.

2. **Revisit a forecast after a month** Before an upcoming event, predict the feeling and duration you expect. Revisit the note after four weeks and compare it with the ordinary details you experienced, without grading the choice as good or bad.
