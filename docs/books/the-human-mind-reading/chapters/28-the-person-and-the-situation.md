# 22. The Person and the Situation {#rl-the-person-and-the-situation}

*Lee Ross and Richard E. Nisbett · 1991 · The Social Mind*

**Behavior reflects the person interacting with circumstances.**

::resource{id="thm-the-person-and-the-situation"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

An employee stays quiet in a rushed meeting with unfamiliar senior colleagues. The next day, given time to prepare for a smaller workshop, the same employee suggests three workable changes. A manager who saw only the first meeting might have mistaken circumstances for a stable trait.

## What distinguishes this book

Lee Ross and Richard Nisbett examine how settings, roles and expectations shape actions that observers attribute to personality. Their account encourages a second look at the situation before assigning a lasting label to a person.

## What the claim does not establish

Situations and dispositions both matter. A single change in behavior does not establish that character is irrelevant or that responsibility disappears.

> **Read alongside Behave.** Ross and Nisbett focus on social explanations and attribution. Sapolsky adds biological and developmental timescales. The wider causal picture should increase accuracy, not provide a simple excuse for harmful conduct.

## Eight ideas

1. **Context changes action** A person may act differently in two settings. Explaining the difference requires knowledge of both circumstances, not a personality label alone.

2. **Dispositions are not destiny** A quiet meeting does not prove a quiet personality. Compare behavior across settings before treating one observation as a lasting trait.

3. **Observers underrate circumstances** Observers often give too much weight to character and too little to circumstances. This error is especially tempting when the context is hidden.

4. **Social roles shift decisions** Assigned roles change what behavior is rewarded or permitted. A role does not erase the responsibility for the action.

5. **Norms create local expectations** Local norms signal what others expect. A behavior that appears personal may also follow a routine of the group.

6. **Small differences matter** Small changes to choices or surroundings can alter results. An apparently minor instruction may have practical consequences.

7. **Perception filters explanations** Observers often notice a person's action before the constraints around it. Ask which parts of the setting the observer could not see.

8. **Prediction needs situational details** Predicting behavior improves when we ask what constraints and opportunities someone faced. Labels alone contain little situational information.

## Two things to try

1. **List constraints before judging** Take a missed deadline or quiet meeting as an example. List three situational constraints that might explain it and identify what information you would need to check each.

2. **Change a meeting condition** For a future meeting, change one condition such as advance questions or group size. Observe participation without using one meeting to assign a lasting personality label.
