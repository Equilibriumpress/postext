# How We Think {#theme-thinking}

Two members of a hiring panel read the same application. One remembers the candidate's confident opening sentence. The other notices that the evidence for a key qualification is weak. Both leave the room feeling certain. Neither has yet compared their assumptions.

Judgment is not a single error to be repaired. A fast impression, an unnoticed detail and unwanted disagreement between reviewers are different problems. A correction that helps with one may do nothing for the others.

Kahneman explains shortcuts that make a plausible answer feel complete. Chabris and Simons show what attention can leave outside awareness. Noise asks whether different decision-makers reach inconsistent answers from identical evidence. Galef and McRaney then ask how a belief is defended, questioned or revised. Together, these books offer different safeguards rather than one prescription for rationality.

## Five connected readings

- :ref{id="rl-thinking-fast-and-slow" text="Thinking, Fast and Slow"} — Fast impressions need a deliberate second question, not blanket distrust of intuition.
- :ref{id="rl-the-invisible-gorilla" text="The Invisible Gorilla"} — An unexpected detail can escape a focused observer.
- :ref{id="rl-noise" text="Noise"} — Independent ratings reveal disagreement that one decision conceals.
- :ref{id="rl-the-scout-mindset" text="The Scout Mindset"} — Calibration means allowing evidence to lower confidence.
- :ref{id="rl-how-minds-change" text="How Minds Change"} — Curiosity about another person's reasons is different from trying to win.

**A necessary distinction.** Slowing down is not a universal cure. Extra thought can reinforce a familiar explanation unless someone checks what information is missing.

**Carry this question forward:** What evidence would make you change a judgment you have already explained to someone else?
