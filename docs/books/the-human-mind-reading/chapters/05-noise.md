# 03. Noise {#rl-noise}

*Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass R. Sunstein · 2021 · How We Think*

**Inconsistent evaluations require different remedies from systematic bias.**

::resource{id="thm-noise"}

## In context

*DOCUMENTED EXAMPLE*

A team of insurance underwriters evaluates the same realistic risk cases independently. Their estimates differ more than their organization expects. The case, reported by Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, illustrates how variation becomes visible only when comparable judgments are collected together.

[Research/case source](https://behavioralscientist.org/a-conversation-with-daniel-kahneman-about-noise/)

## What distinguishes this book

Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein separate bias, a directional tendency, from noise, unwanted spread across judgments. Their contribution is an institutional method: gather independent decisions, examine variation and redesign the process before blaming individual reviewers.

## What the claim does not establish

The insurance case is reported by an author and is not, by itself, an independently replicated estimate of variation in every profession. Subjective or creative assessments need not converge on one answer.

> **Read alongside Thinking, Fast and Slow.** An anchor can pull multiple people in one direction. Noise can remain even if the average judgment looks reasonable. A useful audit therefore checks both systematic error and the spread between independent answers.

## Eight ideas

1. **Bias and noise are different errors** Bias is a repeated tilt in one direction. Noise is the spread of judgments that should agree, and the two can occur together.

2. **Judges disagree on similar cases** Compare judgments only when reviewers face genuinely comparable cases and criteria. Disagreement on different questions is not automatically unwanted noise.

3. **Mood can alter a judgment** A judgment made after a difficult day may differ from one made under calm conditions. Institutions rarely measure these shifts.

4. **Sequence affects assessments** When cases arrive in a particular order, preceding cases can influence the next rating. Shuffle or isolate inputs where possible.

5. **Structured criteria reduce variation** Clear decision criteria keep irrelevant details from driving evaluations. A checklist is not enough unless users apply it consistently.

6. **Independent judgments avoid group anchoring** Independent estimates preserve useful disagreement before a group conversation creates pressure to align with the first speaker.

7. **Decision hygiene improves consistency** Decision hygiene preserves independent ratings and checks shared criteria before discussion. Its value depends on a domain where consistency matters.

8. **Measure disagreement before fixing it** Record the spread of estimates before designing a remedy. Average agreement alone can hide systematic differences between evaluators.

## Two things to try

1. **Compare two independent ratings** Ask two reviewers to assess the same anonymized example with identical criteria and a shared scale. Collect the scores independently, then compare both the numerical gap and reasons given.

2. **Set the criteria before reading** Before reading the next application or proposal, choose three criteria and define how each will be scored. After the decision, check whether each criterion actually guided the rating.
