# 07. Remember {#rl-remember}

*Lisa Genova · 2021 · Memory & Learning*

**A lapse in recall is not, by itself, a sign of disease.**

::resource{id="thm-remember"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

A host introduces three new colleagues while thinking about an upcoming talk. Later, one name is missing. The lapse may have begun at the introduction, when little attention was available for encoding. The situation is familiar, but it cannot establish what caused any individual's memory problem.

## What distinguishes this book

Lisa Genova explains everyday remembering through attention, encoding, retrieval and forgetting. Her useful distinction is between information that never became a stable memory and information that is difficult to recover at a particular moment.

## What the claim does not establish

A classroom-style example is not a diagnosis. Persistent, progressive or concerning memory changes deserve a professional assessment rather than reassurance from a general-interest book.

> **Read alongside Make It Stick.** Genova explains why a name or event may be hard to remember. Make It Stick tests study methods intended to improve later access to learning. Both value retrieval, but they address different questions and should not be combined into a clinical promise.

## Eight ideas

1. **Attention gates memory formation** Memory begins with attention. A name heard while planning the next sentence may never be encoded strongly enough to retrieve.

2. **Forgetting is a normal brain process** A distracted introduction may leave little information to retrieve later. Give the name and the person your attention before choosing a memory trick.

3. **Sleep supports consolidation** Sleep supports memory consolidation, although its effects vary across tasks. A single poor night does not define someone's capacity.

4. **Association aids retrieval** A new fact is easier to retrieve when linked to a distinctive image or meaning. A random repetition has fewer retrieval paths.

5. **Emotion affects what is retained** Emotion changes what stands out during an event. A vivid central detail can coexist with poor recall for surrounding information.

6. **Stress can obstruct recall** Stress affects attention and retrieval in complex ways. Temporary blankness under pressure does not show what a person truly knows.

7. **Cues help recover details** Forgetting can follow weak encoding, interference or a retrieval problem. One missed name alone cannot tell you which explanation applies.

8. **Memory is not a perfect recording** Remembering involves reconstruction. A sincere and vivid recollection is not a perfect recording of the past.

## Two things to try

1. **Give a name your attention** When meeting someone, pause other tasks and listen to their name. Repeat it naturally, link it with a detail from the conversation and see whether you remember it that evening.

2. **Set a reliable reminder** Choose one calendar or reminder tool and record the next real appointment, including its date and a timely alert. Review the tool at the same point in your routine for one week.
