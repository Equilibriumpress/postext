# 21. Influence {#rl-influence}

*Robert B. Cialdini · originally 1984; New and Expanded edition 2021 · The Social Mind*

**Social cues guide agreement and persuasion.**

::resource{id="thm-influence"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

A community organizer offers a small gift, mentions respected supporters and says only a few places remain at an event. The invitation also appeals to shared membership. A newcomer feels drawn to agree but has not yet learned whether the event suits her. Each cue affects the invitation without proving its value.

[Context reference](https://cialdini.com/books)

## What distinguishes this book

Robert Cialdini catalogs recurring social cues that influence agreement. The six familiar principles belong to the original work, while the New and Expanded 2021 edition adds unity, the appeal of shared identity.

## What the claim does not establish

The use of influence principles is not inherently manipulative, and no single cue determines a decision. Ethical persuasion keeps relevant information visible and refusal genuinely possible.

> **Read alongside The Righteous Mind.** Cialdini examines why people agree to a request in a particular social setting. Haidt asks why people assign different moral importance to a proposal. Persuasion by shared identity is distinct from a reasoned disagreement about fairness.

## Eight ideas

1. **Reciprocity creates obligation** A gift or favor may encourage a return gesture. The original kindness does not obligate a person to accept an unrelated request.

2. **Commitment encourages consistency** A past public commitment may make a later yes feel consistent. Before agreeing again, ask whether the new request still serves the original purpose.

3. **Social proof offers a shortcut** Other people's actions help under uncertainty. A crowd can also repeat the same mistake or respond to fabricated popularity.

4. **Authority influences decisions** Credentials and roles can signal expertise. Check whether an authority is relevant to the particular claim being made.

5. **Liking softens resistance** Similarity, familiarity and personal warmth affect receptiveness. Warmth and evidence should still be evaluated separately.

6. **Scarcity increases urgency** Limited availability makes an option feel valuable. Pressure to decide quickly should trigger a check of the underlying value.

7. **Unity signals shared identity** A shared identity can make an invitation persuasive, especially in the expanded edition. Belonging offers no independent proof of the claim.

8. **Persuasion has ethical boundaries** Social influence occurs in ordinary life. Fair use involves clear information, voluntary choice and respect for refusal.

## Two things to try

1. **Assess value without urgency** Choose a recent offer promoted as scarce or popular. List concrete reasons to accept or decline while excluding the countdown, endorsement and social pressure from your first judgment.

2. **Check one authority claim** Find one factual claim made by someone you like or respect. Check the evidence through a separate reliable source and record whether the claim stands without the person's reputation.
