# 19. Drive {#rl-drive}

*Daniel H. Pink · 2009 · Habits & Motivation*

**Motivation has different drivers for routine and creative tasks.**

::resource{id="thm-drive"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

Two teams prepare the same report. One receives a sequence of instructions for each paragraph. The other has a clear deadline and evidence standard, but chooses its method and receives useful feedback. Some members work more confidently with that discretion. The contrast is illustrative, not a test of productivity.

## What distinguishes this book

Daniel Pink organizes research on motivation around autonomy, mastery and purpose. His central question is how work design and meaningful progress affect engagement, especially when a task requires judgment rather than mere repetition.

## What the claim does not establish

Different tasks and workers respond differently to rewards, autonomy and feedback. The three-part framework is not a formula that predicts motivation in every workplace.

> **Read alongside Scarcity.** Pink asks what helps people engage when meaningful choices are possible. Mullainathan and Shafir ask what happens when time, money and administrative burdens narrow those choices. Advice about autonomy loses force when the conditions for choosing are absent.

## Eight ideas

1. **Control can weaken intrinsic interest** Close supervision and contingent rewards can narrow attention in some tasks. Their effects vary by context, complexity and timing.

2. **Choice promotes ownership** A meaningful choice about method can support ownership when goals remain clear. Unlimited freedom without support is not the same as autonomy.

3. **Skill grows through practice** Mastery develops through practice at a manageable level of difficulty. Progress involves effort and useful information about mistakes.

4. **Purpose gives direction** A meaningful purpose connects a task to a contribution. A slogan is insufficient when the task itself conflicts with stated aims.

5. **Rewards differ by task** Rewards can support some routine activities but crowd out interest in others. Avoid treating every kind of work as identical.

6. **Feedback guides improvement** Timely, specific feedback identifies what to improve. Praise without information is a weak substitute for learning.

7. **Mastery requires persistence** Mastery depends on feedback and repeated attempts, not a constant feeling of progress. Build time to improve a skill into the work itself.

8. **Supportive settings enable autonomy** A workplace can offer choices, development and a clear reason for work. Organizational constraints determine how much room is available.

## Two things to try

1. **Offer a meaningful choice of method** Identify a shared task with clear outcome criteria. Offer a genuine choice between two suitable approaches, then ask whether the choice helped the work without lowering standards.

2. **Track improvement with feedback** Choose a skill that matters for the same work. Define one observable sign of improvement and ask for feedback after the next attempt rather than relying on praise alone.
