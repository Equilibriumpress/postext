# 23. Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me) {#rl-mistakes-were-made}

*Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson · 2007 · The Social Mind*

**Self-justification rewrites the narrative around a poor choice.**

::resource{id="thm-mistakes-were-made"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

A planning team spends months developing a proposal. New figures show rising costs, but its members keep repeating the optimistic arguments from the first meeting. One member suggests comparing the remaining benefits with the remaining costs. That question is difficult because abandoning the plan would also mean revising an earlier public claim.

## What distinguishes this book

Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson examine self-justification and cognitive dissonance: how people protect a coherent view of themselves after contradictory evidence appears. The book shows why admitting a mistaken decision often carries a social and personal cost.

## What the claim does not establish

Escalation of commitment and sunk-cost reasoning overlap with, but are not identical to, cognitive dissonance. Continued investment is sometimes rational when future benefits exceed future costs.

> **Read alongside The Scout Mindset.** Tavris and Aronson explain why defending an earlier decision feels appealing. Galef asks what practices help someone revise a belief despite that pressure. Recognizing self-justification is a start, not proof that every disputed decision is wrong.

## Eight ideas

1. **Dissonance motivates explanations** An action that conflicts with a person's self-image creates discomfort. A convenient explanation may restore consistency without correcting the mistake.

2. **Commitment hardens a position** Each public defense can make retreat feel more costly. Reassess the remaining evidence without treating earlier statements as binding.

3. **Memory supports self-image** Memory tends to support the story we tell about ourselves. Repeated retelling may strengthen that interpretation rather than its accuracy.

4. **Groups defend shared identity** A shared identity makes criticism feel personal. Groups can defend a position even when it harms the original goal.

5. **Escalation hides sunk costs** Sunk costs are unrecoverable, but they often shape the next decision. Ask what you would choose if starting today.

6. **Admission can repair errors** An honest admission describes what happened and what will change. It is different from performing guilt without repair.

7. **Humility aids correction** A correction is easier to make when it does not require a complete public humiliation. Separate the error from a judgment about character.

8. **Evidence should outrank pride** A record made before outcomes are known can expose retrospective excuses. Evidence should remain available after the result.

## Two things to try

1. **Set a rule for revising a choice** For one pending decision, write the expected benefits and a specific observation that would trigger review. Keep the rule available when progress becomes difficult to evaluate.

2. **Admit an error and plan a repair** Choose a minor past mistake. Describe the decision, the evidence you overlooked and one practical correction, without calling yourself or anyone else a bad person.
