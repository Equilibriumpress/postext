# 12. Emotional Agility {#rl-emotional-agility}

*Susan David · 2016 · Emotions & Happiness*

**Make room for an emotion while choosing a values-led response.**

::resource{id="thm-emotional-agility"}

## In context

*ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO*

After presenting a proposal, a designer receives blunt criticism and feels ashamed. Her first impulse is to avoid the next meeting. Instead, she writes down the useful part of the feedback and chooses one revision because learning matters to her. The discomfort does not instantly disappear.

## What distinguishes this book

Susan David's emphasis is psychological flexibility: making room for difficult feelings, examining the story attached to them and acting in line with chosen values. Her advice concerns response, not the elimination of unwanted emotions.

## What the claim does not establish

Emotional labeling is not, by itself, a treatment. Values cannot remove discrimination, unsafe work or persistent distress, and exercises should not be presented as clinical alternatives.

> **Read alongside How Emotions Are Made.** David begins where Barrett's theory leaves a practical question. Even if the origin of an emotion is uncertain, a person can examine the next response. That practical choice is separate from proving how the feeling arose.

## Eight ideas

1. **Emotions communicate concerns** A feeling may signal an unmet need or a concern, but it does not dictate the right action. Listen before choosing a response.

2. **Naming feelings creates room** Naming a feeling precisely creates a moment for reflection. It does not require suppressing the feeling or accepting every thought attached to it.

3. **Thoughts are not commands** A thought can arrive uninvited without being an order. Describing it as a thought creates useful distance from its content.

4. **Avoidance can narrow behavior** Pushing uncomfortable emotions away can make them harder to approach. The aim is willingness, not endless analysis or forced positivity.

5. **Values guide selected actions** Values are enduring directions, unlike passing moods. A small action can express a value even during a difficult day.

6. **Self-compassion supports change** Responding kindly to a setback avoids turning a mistake into a fixed judgment about character. Accountability still matters.

7. **Perspective changes emotional grip** A values-based action may still feel uncomfortable. Judge the step by whether it serves a meaningful aim, not by immediate relief.

8. **Small commitments sustain movement** A change that matters need not start with a dramatic promise. Repeated manageable steps test what works in ordinary conditions.

## Two things to try

1. **Name the feeling without obeying it** During a manageable everyday difficulty, name the feeling and the action it urges. Put both into words before deciding whether following the impulse serves you.

2. **Choose a small values-based action** Choose a value relevant to the same situation, such as fairness or learning. Take one feasible action within five minutes and note whether you acted consistently with the value, even if the feeling stayed.
