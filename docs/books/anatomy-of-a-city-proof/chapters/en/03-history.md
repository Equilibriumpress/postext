03 / HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT

:::

# Growth in stages {style="display-heading"}

:::frame{slot="art"}

::resource{id="growth-timeline-vertical"}

:::

:::frame{slot="body-a"}

**Before 1600.** Amsterdam grew around the Amstel and its connections with other waterways. In the medieval town, canals served defensive and economic purposes. The relationship between water and urban growth preceded the celebrated canal belt.

**The first major extension.** In 1585 a new canal was added near the location of today's Herengracht. This earlier expansion helps explain why the historic centre contains waterways of different periods and functions.

**1613 onwards.** A larger western extension created the first section of the planned canal belt alongside the Jordaan. Development proceeded in stages, combining new infrastructure with the sale and development of building plots.

:::

:::frame{slot="body-b"}

**The later seventeenth century.** Further expansion extended the canal system across the Amstel. Rather than a single act of construction, the district emerged through successive decisions about land, infrastructure and building.

**A legacy of trade.** The canal belt records Amsterdam's transformation into a major trading port. Its merchant houses and warehouses belong to an economy connected to distant markets, colonial territories and systems of coerced labour.

**2010 and after.** UNESCO recognised the seventeenth-century canal ring within the Singelgracht as a World Heritage property. The designation concerns the urban ensemble, including the spatial arrangement, rather than only the architectural merit of individual houses. Preserving a living district requires attention to changes in use as well as appearance.

# Three urban patterns {style="display-heading"}

:::frame{slot="lead"}

Street geometry changes the way routes connect. These three diagrams isolate different organising principles.

:::

:::frame{slot="art"}

::resource{id="urban-patterns"}

:::

:::frame{slot="body-a"}

**The ring.** Curved routes organise movement around a centre. Radial streets connect successive rings. In Amsterdam the water and the older city strongly influenced the geometry. Distances depend on where the crossings lie.

**The grid.** Repeating intersections divide land into blocks. A grid tends to offer alternative routes and makes blocks relatively easy to subdivide. Barcelona's planned Eixample provides a useful contrasting example, though its diagonal avenues and chamfered blocks complicate a pure geometric grid.

Neither pattern determines travel behaviour on its own. The distribution of destinations and the design of crossings are equally important.

:::

:::frame{slot="body-b"}

**The organic network.** Streets grow around existing routes, property boundaries and terrain. Junctions vary in shape and spacing. Edinburgh's Old Town illustrates a relationship between a principal route along a ridge and smaller connecting passages.

**Compare three properties.** First, trace routes between opposite edges. Second, count the choices offered at intersections. Third, look for the relationship between streets and building plots.

These are abstract patterns, not measured maps of the named cities. Actual urban networks combine geometries from different periods. A fair numerical comparison would need geographic data at a consistent scale and a clear definition of the study area.

:::

:::frame{slot="concept"}

**Connectivity:** the ease of movement between places in a network.
