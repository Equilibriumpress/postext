# A planned extension {style="display-heading"}

:::frame{slot="lead"}

A series of curved waterways surrounds the older centre and links the city to its waterfront.

:::

:::frame{slot="body-a"}

**Planning the extension.** During the seventeenth century Amsterdam expanded through a coordinated programme of waterways, streets and building plots. The scheme linked port activity to residential development. Public authorities established the main spatial framework, while individual owners developed their plots. This division of responsibility helps explain why the canal district has both a coherent plan and a diverse collection of houses.

The Jordaan developed beside the western canal belt, with a distinct street pattern and a different social and economic character. Comparing the two districts reveals how a single expansion programme accommodated different activities and households.

Commercial prosperity also depended on colonial trade and exploitation, including the Atlantic slave trade. Reading the physical city without acknowledging these connections leaves an incomplete account of its history.

:::

:::frame{slot="body-b"}

**A connected network.** The curves establish long, recognisable corridors. Bridges and cross streets connect the rings. A person travelling along a canal encounters a succession of intersections, while someone crossing the district moves repeatedly between water and land.

A bridge is more than a crossing. Its position affects walking routes, views along the canal and the accessibility of neighbouring buildings. Where crossings are sparse, even destinations close in a straight line require a longer journey.

The building plots along the canal convert the large urban plan into a succession of individual properties. At ground level, the repeated entrances and façades make this underlying structure visible. The contrast between regular infrastructure and varied buildings remains a useful way to understand the district.

:::

:::frame{slot="concept"}

**Urban structure:** continuous corridors, transverse crossings and individual plots.

# From water to house {style="display-heading"}

:::frame{slot="lead"}

A narrow strip of public space separates the canal from the buildings. Below ground, foundations carry their loads.

:::

:::frame{slot="art"}

::resource{id="street-section-annotated"}

:::

:::frame{slot="caption"}

Conceptual section, not a surveyed engineering drawing.

:::

:::frame{slot="body-a"}

**The public edge.** A quay wall retains the soil behind the canal edge. The street above serves buildings, pedestrians, bicycles and other traffic. Trees, moorings and street furniture occupy parts of the same corridor.

The condition of the wall therefore matters beyond the water itself. A damaged structure affects street access and the neighbouring public space. Repair involves balancing construction access, water traffic and the needs of residents.

:::

:::frame{slot="body-b"}

**The ground beneath.** Amsterdam's soft soils require special attention to foundations. Historic buildings, quays and bridges frequently rely on timber piles driven into deeper supporting ground.

A building and a quay have different structural tasks, even when they stand close together. The building foundation carries its weight, while the quay retains the ground beside the canal. Groundwater conditions also matter to historic wooden foundations. The diagram isolates these relationships without implying a standard foundation depth.
